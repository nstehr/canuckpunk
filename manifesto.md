# Canuckpunk - The Manifesto 🇨🇦

> People die. Institutions remember.

## Main Premise

Canuckpunk is a multiplayer world set in an alternate Canada where the institutions that built the country never disappeared. 

- Hudson's Bay Company grew and thrived.
- Railways expanded and continue to be a key element
- Telegraphs are the key communication backbone. They didn't survive as the technology, they survived as the _protocol_

Imagine a world where the future was inspired by civil engineers rather than bankers and venture capitalists.

The technology evolved but the institutions remained, and over 300 years later no one can free us from any of it. None of it was intended for an immortal bureaucracy. No one knows how to break it.

## Why Canada
Canada inherited its institutions before it had a national identity.
Long before Confederation, people traded through companies, worshipped in churches, kept parish registers, and surveyed boundaries, all of it connected by rivers and portage routes. The railway came later and bound the country together.
The country grew around those institutions rather than replacing them.
Canuckpunk asks what happens when those institutions never stopped gathering responsibility, and no one built any system for handing it back.

## Memory

The Company remembers.
The Church remembers.
The Survey remembers.
The Registry remembers.
Indigenous communities remember.
Local people remember.
Precedent remembers.

The conflict isn't over power. It's over whose memory becomes authoritative.

There are three kinds of memory in this world:

- **Machine memory**: instant, total, and silently amendable. Everything the engines hold.
- **Paper memory**: slow, human-verified, hard to reach and hard to alter. What was never migrated.
- **Human memory**: unrecorded, uncitable, and dies with the body. What nobody wrote down.

Every dispute in this world is a fight between two of them.

## Theme

Authority over memory is worth fighting over because stability is indispensable and no one can opt out of it. The institutions aren't villains. They are load-bearing. That is what makes them dangerous.

The main question is not whether the institutions are good or bad, but whether their inherent desire to preserve stability can ever change.

The people have little say in where the country goes next.

## The Institutions

- The Company (HBC) keeps food and goods in production and moving
- The Railway keeps the country connected
- The Survey keeps land coherent
- The Grid keeps the lights on
- The Registry knows who you are and where you came from
- The Church existed before all of it. The Church isn't "the religious faction." It's another immortal institution with a different memory. It is the only memory that was never migrated. Still paper, still ink, still by hand, still en français. It can't be queried at machine speed, and it can't be silently amended at machine speed either. It is slow in both directions.

## Institutional Intelligence

Institutions over 300 years old need to scale beyond human scale. They are governed by artificial institutional intelligence. Think of them less as your sci-fi AI and more as a precedent engine. They don't optimize. They resolve problems by finding the nearest neighbour and applying it.

- SIR JOHN sets the government continuity
- LEDGER remembers all business transactions big or small
- BENCHMARK runs the Survey
- CONDUCTOR keeps trains moving
- CURRENT keeps the lights on

Institutional intelligence produces stability and rationality. It produces continuity. Not power. Continuity is simply what falls out of resolving every question by precedent.

## History

There is no one agreed upon measure of time.

- The Company measures time from its Royal Charter
- The Government measures from Confederation
- Surveyors think in revisions to their plan
- Railways think in eras
- Power engineers refer to generations of their infrastructure

When a Company clerk says "Charter 376" and a government official replies "Confederation 159," neither is correcting the other.

## The Player

A player is a professional, not a chosen hero:

- A surveyor
- A government auditor
- A railway inspector
- A telegraph engineer
- A voyageur
- A systems analyst

The player changes the world through understanding the systems rather than inherited destiny.

For example:

- Re-routing freight can save or eliminate a community
- Updating the ledger can restore a once forgotten property
- A missing registry record can remove a person from society

## The Punks

Someone whose Registry record disappeared long ago.

They can't:

- own land
- receive wages
- buy rail tickets
- inherit property
- vote
- appear in official statistics

But they still exist. They live at the far end of the memory spectrum, with nothing but human memory, held by the people who were present.

That produces a society:

- Debts are held in memory by witnesses. A debt is only as real as the people who remember it.
- Reputation can't be transferred, audited, or inherited. It dies with the person who earned it.
- Nothing accumulates. Punk society has to re-establish itself every generation, because it has no way to hand anything down.
- Trust is the only ledger, and it doesn't scale past the number of people you can know.

The punks and the Church are structural allies for opposite reasons. Both hold memory the engines cannot touch.

## Death

When your character dies, the institution appoints a replacement who inherits the office, the unresolved work, and the institutional reputation. Your file remains.

People still mourn death. It is not routine. But the institution will fill your role by Monday.

There is knowledge of the previous:

- "The last surveyor promised this would be fixed"
- "You are the fifth auditor we've had in these parts"

Imagine a player discovering a corrupt official, then dying on the railways. 20 years later a player finds a notebook from the previous character. The institution remembers.

Or a player who is an auditor that discovers rail freight discrepancies. They die. Their next character joins the Railway and finds the issue from the other side.

The successor inherits the _file_ but not the _memory_. The player remembers that the district manager falsified the freight logs. The new character can't cite it. It isn't in the record.

A punk dying is the ultimate death. No file, no inheritance, no continuity. The worst punishment in this world is being removed from the record. Losing it mid-career, keeping the character, and discovering what that costs is more frightening than dying.

## Conflict

This isn't a revolution by violence and rifle. It is about understanding how the machine works.

- Work within the institutions, or
- Sabotage them, or
- Abandon them completely, or
- Attempt to replace them

## Tone

Concrete, snow, steel, boreal forest, prairies, coastal. Historic buildings surrounded by AI datacenters and quantum computing. Railways with maglevs, and constant surveying and forming of the land.

One key thing is to make sure we don't erase regional differences. Canadians don't refer to things the same way. Ontario and Quebec refer to Hydro and drink milk from a bag. Alberta calls it "Power," or the name of the power company, and uses words like "coulee." We need it to feel Canadian to someone who is from Canada and unique in general to people not from Canada. Preserve local language, history, and culture.

- Someone in Winnipeg: "Hydro's doing maintenance."
- Someone from Alberta: "Power's down again."
- A Company employee: "CURRENT has shed load across District Four."
- A bureaucrat: "The Grid has issued an advisory."

## Design Philosophy

When adding new functionality (mechanics, quests, factions, features, etc.) consider:

- What institution does this belong to
- What problem was it originally trying to solve
- How have hundreds of years of accumulation changed it
- How would someone on the inside describe the world
- How does the mechanic enforce that ideas are remembered by institutions longer than by people

Does this explore institutions? Then yes, it belongs. If no, then it probably belongs to a different game.
